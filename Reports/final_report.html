Seven power modes, twenty-nine hours: mapping a passive heatsink's limits

Every nvpmodel power mode on two NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano boards, each driven for four continuous hours at 100 % CPU, ~97 % GPU and a sustained memory load. One board is conduction-cooled — no fan, no fins, just a copper braid to a remote aluminium plate; the other keeps its stock active cooling.

Not one mode throttled, and not one run aborted. The passively cooled Orin held its maximum clock through every configuration up to uncapped MAXN_SUPER at 18 W, peaking at 89.8 °C — 9.2 °C below the 99 °C throttle trip. The modified heatsink is sufficient across the module's entire power range.

The two thermal solutions under test

The boards differ in more than power budget — they represent two fundamentally different cooling strategies. Every comparison in this report should be read with that in mind.

What the data says

The passive heatsink never became the limiting factor

Across a 32 °C span from 7 W (58.0 °C) to uncapped MAXN_SUPER (89.8 °C), the Orin held maximum CPU clock in every mode — 99.9 % of samples at 1728 MHz even at its hottest. Clock, not temperature, is the honest pass criterion: silicon protects itself by downclocking, so a board can look cool precisely because it quietly gave up performance. That never happened here.

Stability degrades sharply above 15 W — the real warning sign

The final-hour spread tells the story the peak temperature hides: 0.94 °C at 7 W, 2.34 °C at 15 W, but 9.16 °C at 25 W and 12.28 °C at MAXN_SUPER. In the high-power modes the junction swings by more than 10 °C on ordinary room airflow alone. Nothing throttled, but the margin has become dependent on ambient conditions rather than on the heatsink. For deployment, 15 W is where this thermal solution stops having comfortable headroom.

A one-hour test would have given the wrong answer

At MAXN_SUPER the board appeared to plateau near 81 °C for a full hour, then climbed again to ~89 °C between the 60- and 100-minute marks — a +6.4 °C late rise measured from the 30-minute mark, with the same delayed ramp at 25 W (+3.6 °C). The braid-and-remote-plate construction is exactly why: the spreader plate is a large thermal capacitor charged through the braid's fixed conductance, so the junction settles at an apparent plateau long before the plate itself is saturated. Any soak shorter than ~2 hours will systematically flatter this design.

7W is effectively free thermally

The Orin's 7 W mode reached its peak of 58.0 °C sixteen minutes in and never exceeded it across the remaining three and three-quarter hours — the tightest final-hour spread in the matrix at 0.94 °C, with post-ramp drift of just +0.80 °C. At 6.85 W with two cores gated off, the heatsink sheds heat about as fast as the SoC produces it.

Cooldown rate scales with stored energy, not cooling quality

Decay ranged from 0.50 °C/min at 7 W to 2.30 °C/min at MAXN_SUPER. The hotter modes shed faster simply because a larger gradient to ambient drives more transfer. Earlier fitting of the decay curve gave a thermal time constant of ~12.8 min for the passive Orin against ~12.0 min for the fan-cooled J3010 — the modified heatsink sheds heat at almost exactly the rate of stock active cooling.

What this data does not establish

Ambient room temperature was never instrumented, so absolute figures are not reproducible — only the deltas transfer. The J3010's INA3221 rails are permission-gated under JetPack 5, so it has no power telemetry at all. And the two boards are not comparable to each other: different power caps, 8 vs 4 SMs, 8 GB vs 4 GB, passive vs active cooling. Each board's own rise-above-idle and clock stability are the meaningful numbers.

Method

Load was applied with stress-ng — six CPU workers (four in the core-gated modes) cycling all stressor methods, plus a memory worker capped at 25 % of total RAM so the 4 GB board could not be pushed into the OOM killer. GPU load came from a purpose-built CUDA kernel (gpu_burn.cu), a register-resident fused-multiply-add chain sized to each device's SM count and compiled on-device with nvcc — deliberately compute-bound rather than memory-bound, since that is what drives GPU power and therefore heat.

Telemetry was sampled every 5 seconds from tegrastats and sysfs simultaneously — 46 columns on the Orin, 44 on the J3010 — covering every thermal zone, per-core utilisation and clock, GPU and memory-bus load, power rails and fan state. Every row was flushed and fsynced individually so a crash at hour three would still leave three hours of usable data. An automatic abort at 99 °C was armed for all seven runs and never fired.

Between modes the board waited on a temperature gate rather than a fixed timer — exiting once the trailing five-minute slope flattened below 0.4 °C, bounded to 25–50 minutes. A fixed wait would have let overnight ambient drift bias later modes, since each run would start from a different thermal state. Trip points were read from each kernel rather than taken from datasheets: both boards report passive at 99 °C and critical at 104 °C on every zone.

The Orin's 7 W mode required a reboot to apply, because it takes CPU cores 4 and 5 offline and JetPack 6.2 will not do that live. It was set with nvpmodel -m 3 --force and verified after boot against the config — 4 cores online, 960 MHz CPU cap, 408 MHz GPU cap — before the soak began.
