Four hours at full load: NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano thermal soak

Two Orin Nano boards driven to 100 % CPU, 97 % GPU and a sustained memory load for four continuous hours — one on a modified passive heatsink with the fan removed, one on stock active cooling. Junction temperature, per-core clocks and power sampled every 5 seconds.

02:48 → 06:57 IST 5,911 samples 5 s interval 0 throttle events 0 aborts

Both boards completed the full soak without throttling. The passively cooled Orin Super peaked at 71.8 °C — 27.2 °C below the 99 °C throttle trip — and held a flat plateau for the final three and a half hours. The modified heatsink reaches thermal equilibrium at this load and stays there.

Reading the results

The plateau is the finding, not the peak

A peak temperature on its own says little — it could be a number still climbing when the clock ran out. The Orin rose 13.9 °C in total, but almost all of that happened in the first half hour: measured from t+30 min to the end of the soak it moved only +1.16 °C in three hours, holding a final-hour spread of 2.34 °C. A heatsink still saturating shows a persistent upward slope; this one flattened. That is what says the passive solution is sufficient for this load indefinitely, not merely for four hours.

Clock stability is the real pass criterion

Both boards held maximum CPU clock for 99.9 % of samples with the GPU pinned near 97 %. This matters more than temperature: silicon protects itself by downclocking, so a board can report comfortable temperatures precisely because it quietly gave up performance. Neither board did. The Orin also held 12.96 W average against its 15 W cap throughout, confirming it stayed at its power ceiling rather than backing off.

These two boards are not directly comparable

The ~11 °C gap between them is not a heatsink comparison. They differ in power cap (15 W vs 10 W), silicon (8 SMs vs 4), memory (8 GB vs 4 GB) and cooling. The J3010 also ran its stock fan throughout. Each board's own rise-above-idle and clock stability are the meaningful figures; the absolute difference between them mostly reflects the 50 % higher power budget the Orin was permitted to burn.

What the data does not cover

Ambient room temperature was not instrumented, so these results are not reproducible in absolute terms — only the deltas are portable. The J3010's INA3221 power rails are permission-gated under JetPack 5, so no power figures exist for it. The cooldown phase was only 5 minutes, enough to establish a decay rate but not a full return to idle.

Headroom suggests a harder test is available

At 15 W with no airflow the Orin used barely half its thermal budget to the 99 °C trip. Re-running at nvpmodel -m 1 (25 W) or -m 2 (MAXN_SUPER) would push the passive heatsink toward its actual limit and give a far more useful characterisation than this run did — this one established that 15 W passive is comfortable, not where the ceiling sits.

Method

Load was applied with stress-ng — six CPU workers cycling all stressor methods, plus one memory worker sized to 25 % of total RAM so the 4 GB board could not be pushed into the OOM killer. GPU load came from a purpose-built CUDA kernel (gpu_burn.cu), a register-resident fused-multiply-add chain sized to each device's SM count, compiled on-device with nvcc. It is deliberately compute-bound rather than memory-bound, since that is what drives GPU power and therefore heat.

Telemetry was sampled every 5 seconds from tegrastats and sysfs simultaneously — 46 columns on the Orin, 44 on the J3010, covering every thermal zone, per-core utilisation and clock, GPU and memory-bus load, power rails and fan state. Each row was flushed and fsynced individually so a crash at hour three would still leave three hours of usable data. An automatic abort at 99 °C was armed throughout and never triggered.

Trip points were read from each kernel rather than taken from datasheets: both boards report passive at 99 °C and critical at 104 °C across every thermal zone.
